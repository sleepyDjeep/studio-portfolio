## What It Is & Why It Matters

Commercial outdoor barrel and cabin kits routinely run `$4,500` to `$9,000`, while naive DIY pallet sheds rely on OSB or plywood roof sheathing that off-gasses formaldehyde resins at `190°F` sauna temperatures and rots out where stove pipes pierce flat roofs. Designed for zero-snow-load South Texas homestead conditions, this blueprint engineers a 4-person (`8' x 8'` exterior, `6' 11" x 6' 11"` finished interior) wood-fired sauna framed, decked, roofed, and benched from standard North American GMA `48" x 40"` **`HT` (Heat-Treated)** or **`KD` (Kiln-Dried)** shipping pallets—without buying a single stick of dimensional `2x6` lumber or a single sheet of plywood.

Every structural splice, rafter spacing interval, and wall rotation was calculated around exact GMA stringer (`48"`) and deck-slat (`40"`) geometry so reclaimed lumber locks together like modular millwork while keeping the overhead corrugated metal roof 100% free of chimney penetrations.

## Real Example: How It Works in Practice

- **Input**: `54–58` clean `HT`-stamped GMA `48" x 40"` pallets (`22` kept intact for the modular floor/wall shell; `32–36` disassembled into `~95` `48"` stringers and `~345` `40"` slats), `9` concrete deck blocks, insect/hardware mesh, `2` sheets of `1/2"` cement board, Rockwool insulation, aluminum foil vapor barrier, corrugated metal roof panels, and a corner wood-burning sauna stove.
- **What Happens**:
  1. **Foundation & Self-Draining Intake Floor (Zone A)**: Three `96"` 3-ply staggered glulam skids (`F-1`, joints supported directly over concrete blocks at `0"`, `48"`, and `96"`, torch-charred *Shou Sugi Ban* style against termites) carry 6 floor pallets narrowed with ripped spacer strips to `1/4"–3/8"` drainage/combustion-air slots over stapled stainless/fiberglass scorpion and mosquito mesh.
  2. **Modular Shell & Zero-Cut Chimney Bay (Zone B)**: Stacking `40"` lower pallets beneath vertically rotated `48"` front-top pallets automatically establishes an `88"` front wall sloping down (`1:12` pitch) to an `80"` rear wall; rotating panel `W-R3` above the stove creates a `17.75"` vertical stringer bay that accepts a `16" x 16"` insulated Class-A horizontal side-wall chimney thimble while cutting **zero load-bearing vertical stringers**.
  3. **Zero-Plywood `19.25"` OC Rafters (Zone C)**: Six `108"` gusseted box-beam rafters (`48"+48"+12"` staggered double stringers sandwiched between `40"` deck-slat side gussets) sit at **`19.25"` on-center** so trimmed **`38.5"`** pallet slats span two full rafter bays and land dead-center on rafters for both solid roof sheathing and interior ceiling cladding.
  4. **Benches & Heat Shield (Zone D)**: An `83"` upper bench (`36"` high, wide enough for 3–4 seated bathers or 1 person stretched flat) pairs with an `18"` step bench and `36"` L-return facing a `36" x 36"` corner stove zone protected by `1"` air-gapped cement board shields.
- **Output**: A weather-tight, self-draining, convective wood-fired outdoor sauna with zero synthetic sheet-good adhesives inside the hot room and zero roof-boot leak points.

## Under the Hood & Local Files

Three engineering decisions stored in our Memory Bank (`M0965`–`M0969`) make 100%-pallet construction structurally sound rather than makeshift:
1. **`19.25"` On-Center Rafter Geometry**: Traditional `24"` OC framing renders `40"` pallet slats useless (`8"` short of spanning two bays, wasting `16"` per slat if spanning one bay). Spacing six rafters at `0"`, `19.25"`, `38.5"`, `57.75"`, `77"`, and `96"` matches `2 x 19.25" = 38.5"` to standard `40"` slats with only `1.5"` of clean end-trim.
2. **Forklift-Notch Compression Orientation**: Standard GMA stringers have two bottom forklift cutouts that weaken bending strength if placed in tension; orienting stringer notches upward (compression edge) or alternating opposing plies locked behind `40"` glued-and-screwed side gussets (`R-1C`) restores full `3.5"` box-beam stiffness.
3. **Passive Under-Floor Stove Draft (`M0968`–`M0969`)**: Drawing cool intake oxygen up through the bug-screened `3/8"` floor slots beneath the wood burner drives natural `loyly` convective circulation over the rocks and benches while letting washdown water drain straight through to gravel below.
