## What It Is & Why It Matters

Inventing an original video game or novel setting from a blank page often produces generic elves, generic cyberpunk megacorps, or years of slow lore drafting before a single mechanic works. Meanwhile, licensing established sci-fi and fantasy franchises costs money and restricts creative freedom. As of **2026**, however, every U.S. book published in **1930 or earlier**—plus dozens of 1930s–1960s pulp novels and Golden Age comics whose 28-year copyright renewals lapsed—sits completely free in the public domain.

We built the **Public-Domain Sci-Fi & Fantasy Mashup Bible** (`25 KB`, stored inside our shared studio library under `storytelling_ideas/`) to turn that legal archive into ready-to-prototype game and fiction blueprints. Instead of adapting a single old novel straight, each entry collides **two or three public-domain worlds with unexpected public-domain characters** so the friction between them generates concrete gameplay mechanics—from light-discipline survival inside an eight-mile metal pyramid to noir murder investigations inside a glass panopticon megacity.

## Real Example: How It Works in Practice

Suppose we want to prototype a first-person survival immersive sim in Godot 4.7 without spending months inventing faction history or risking an IP takedown:

- **Input**: We select two pre-1931 horror/fantasy canons—William Hope Hodgson's *The Night Land* (1912) and *Carnacki the Ghost-Finder* (1910)—and cross-pollinate them with L. Frank Baum's **Nick Chopper / The Tin Woodman** (*The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*, 1900), checking each against our 2026 U.S. copyright and trademark filter.
- **What Happens**: Combining those three ingredients creates **World #1: The Redoubt Protocol**. Millions of years after the Sun dies, humanity's surviving tiers shelter inside the eight-mile-tall Last Redoubt pyramid powered by the fading **Earth Current**. **Current Guild** engineers (modeled on Carnacki) deploy vacuum-tube **Electric Pentacles** to hold back Ab-humans and towering Watchers in the dark, while **The Tin-Bound** (Chopper's order) replace frostbitten limbs with riveted tin prosthetics that grant cold and fear immunity at the cost of rust-blight and lost empathy.
- **Output**: A complete game-design pitch with factions, tone, and three playable systems ready for greyboxing: **Light as a Lethal Trade-Off** (arc-lamps expose ambushers yet wake distant Watchers), **Electric Pentacle** corridor-containment grids, and **Modular Prosthetic Degradation**—all 100% free of licensing fees or trademarked title conflicts.

## Under the Hood & Local Files

### Three-Gate IP Clearance & Trademark Hygiene
- **Gate 1 — Pre-1931 U.S. Public Domain Baseline**: Every work published in **1930 or earlier** is cleared in the United States as of January 1, 2026, unlocking 1930 entrants such as Olaf Stapledon's *Last and First Men* and Dashiell Hammett's original 1930 novel *The Maltese Falcon* alongside pre-1930 Weird Fiction, Victorian sci-fi, and world folklore.
- **Gate 2 — Unrenewed 1923–1963 Pulp & Golden Age Works**: Targets mid-century science fiction (*H. Beam Piper*, *Andre Norton*, *Murray Leinster*) and Golden Age comics (*Fantomah*, *Stardust the Super Wizard*, *The Black Terror*) whose initial 28-year terms expired without renewal, paired with a mandatory pre-release edition check.
- **Gate 3 — Copyright vs. Trademark Separation**: Explicitly separates public-domain story text from active commercial wordmarks. For example, Philip Francis Nowlan's 1928 novella *Armageddon 2419 A.D.* introduces **Anthony Rogers** and Inerton jump-belts in the public domain, while the name *"Buck Rogers"* carries active trademark claims—so *Carcosa in 2419* adapts the 1928 mechanics and protagonist while keeping titles, logos, and store copy 100% clear of trademarked names (applying the same rule to *Tarzan*, *Pellucidar*, *Caspak*, and *Kull*).
